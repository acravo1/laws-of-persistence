# Capability versus Knowledge

## Introduction

Many theories implicitly assume that knowledge and capability are closely related.

In practice, they are not equivalent.

Organizations, systems and individuals frequently possess knowledge without possessing the capability to act upon it.

The distinction is fundamental to the Laws of Persistence.

This document formalizes the difference between knowledge and capability.

---

# The Traditional Assumption

Knowledge is often treated as the ultimate objective of information management.

The common progression is:

    Data
    ↓
    Information
    ↓
    Knowledge
    ↓
    Wisdom

Within this model, knowledge is frequently assumed to imply capability.

However, observation suggests otherwise.


---

# Knowledge

Knowledge represents understanding.

Knowledge answers the question:

    Do we know?

Knowledge allows an individual or organization to explain:

- facts;
- concepts;
- procedures;
- relationships.

Knowledge creates potential.

Knowledge does not guarantee execution.


---

# Capability

Capability represents the practical ability to perform an action.

Capability answers the question:

    Can we act?

Capability requires more than knowledge.

Capability depends upon:

- knowledge;
- resources;
- access;
- context;
- governance;
- recoverability.

Knowledge contributes to capability.

Knowledge does not guarantee capability.


---

# Examples

## Example 1

An organization possesses recovery procedures.

The procedures are documented.

The staff understand them.

No recovery test has ever been executed.

Knowledge exists.

Capability remains uncertain.

---

## Example 2

Source code is preserved.

The original developers have left.

No build environment remains operational.

Knowledge exists.

Capability has degraded.

---

## Example 3

A historical document explains a procedure.

The equipment required to execute the procedure no longer exists.

Knowledge survives.

Capability does not.


---

# The Capability Gap

The difference between what is known and what can actually be performed is defined as the Capability Gap.

Knowledge
        ↓
Capability Gap
        ↓
Capability

The purpose of persistence is to reduce this gap.


---

# Relationship With The Continuity Hypothesis

The Continuity Hypothesis proposes that capability forms a distinct layer above knowledge.

Knowledge answers:

    Do we know?

Capability answers:

    Can we act?

Continuity answers:

    Can we continue acting despite disruption?

The distinction is essential.

Organizations survive through capability.

Not through knowledge alone.


---

# Implications

If capability and knowledge are distinct concepts, organizations should measure more than:

- documentation volume;
- repository size;
- knowledge assets.

They should also evaluate:

- recoverability;
- operational readiness;
- dependency concentration;
- continuity preparedness.

Capability becomes an observable property.


---

# Conclusion

Knowledge creates understanding.

Capability creates action.

The two concepts are related but not equivalent.

A system may possess knowledge while lacking capability.

The objective of persistence is therefore not merely the preservation of knowledge.

It is the preservation of capability.

---

# Foundational Principle

Knowing is not the same as being able to do.
